Theorem 1.1.19. \(\R^I\) is a vector space.

Let \(I\) be a nonempty set.
  1. The set \(\R^I\) together with the vector addition and scalar multiplication operations defined in Definition 1.1.13 is a vector space.
    🔗
    🔗
  2. The zero vector of the vector space \(\R^I\text{,}\) as described by Axiom iii, is the \(I\)-tuple \((0)_{i\in I}\text{:}\) i.e., we have
    \begin{equation*} \boldzero=\boldzero_I=(0)_{i\in I}\text{.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
  3. Given a vector \(\boldx=(x_i)_{i\in I}\text{,}\) its vector inverse \(-\boldx\text{,}\) as described by Axiom iv, is \((-x_i)_{i\in I}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Additionally, we have
    \begin{equation*} -\boldx=(-1)\boldx\text{;} \end{equation*}
    that is, the vector inverse of \(\boldx\) is equal to the scalar multiple \((-1)\boldx\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
🔗
🔗

Proof.

The statement itself of the theorem has already taken care of some of the steps required to verify that something is a vector space. Namely, it has identified the underlying set \(\R^I\) and proposed vector operations, and it has identified the elements of \(\R^I\) that serve as the zero vector and inverse vectors. It remains to show that the eight axioms of Definition 1.1.16 are satisfied. We will verify the existential axioms (Axiom iii-iv), as well as one of the algebraic axioms (Axioms ii and vi), and leave the rest as an exercise.
🔗
Axiom iii.
We claim that \(\boldzero_I=(0)_{i\in I}\) satisfies the identity of Axiom iii. Indeed, for all \(\boldx=(x_i)_{i\in I}\in \R^I\) we have
\begin{align*} \boldzero_I+\boldx \amp =(0)_{i\in I}+(x_i)_{i\in I}\\ \amp =(0+x_i)_{i\in I}\amp \text{(def. of vec. add.)} \\ \amp = (x_i)_{i\in I}\\ \amp =\boldx\text{,} \end{align*}
as desired.
🔗
🔗
Axiom iv.
We claim that given any \(\boldx=(x_i)_{i\in I}\in \R^I\text{,}\) the vector \((-x_i)_{i\in I}\) satisfies the identity of Axiom iv, and thus that \(-\boldx=(-x_i)_{i\in I}\text{.}\) Indeed, we have
\begin{align*} (-x_i)_{i\in I}+(x_i)_{i\in I} \amp =(-x_i+x_i)_{i\in I} \amp \text{(def. of vec. add.)}\\ \amp = (0)_{i\in I}\\ \amp = \boldzero_I\text{,} \end{align*}
as desired.
🔗
🔗
As for the remaining axioms, we will verify Axioms ii and vi, and leave the rest as an exercise. As you see below, the desired identities here all boil down to a familiar property of real number arithmetic: e.g., commutativity of real number addition, real number distributivity, etc. In what follows, \(\boldx=(x_i)_{i\in I}, \boldy=(y_i)_{i\in I}, \boldz=(z_i)_{i\in I}\) will denote arbitrary elements of \(\R^I\text{,}\) and \(c,d\) will denote arbitrary elements of \(\R\text{.}\)
🔗
Axiom ii.
We have
\begin{align*} (\boldx+\boldy)+\boldz \amp = (x_i)_{i\in I}+(y_i)_{i\in I}+\boldz \amp \text{(def. of vec. add.)}\\ \amp = ((x_i)_{i\in I}+(y_i)_{i\in I})+(z_i)_{i\in I} \amp \text{(def. of vec. add.)}\\ \amp = (x_i)_{i\in I}+((y_i)_{i\in I}+(z_i)_{i\in I}) \amp \text{(assoc. of real add.)}\\ \amp =\boldx+(\boldy+\boldz) \text{.} \end{align*}
🔗
🔗
Axiom vi.
For all \(c,d\in \R\) and \(\boldx\in \R^I\text{,}\) we have
\begin{align*} (c+d)\boldx \amp = ((c+d)x_i)_{i\in I} \amp \text{(def. of sc. mult.)} \\ \amp =(cx_i+dx_i)_{i\in I} \amp \text{(dist. prop. of reals)}\\ \amp =(cx_i)_{i\in I}+(dx_i)_{i\in I} \amp \text{(def. of vec. add.)}\\ \amp =c\boldx+d\boldx \amp \text{(def. of sc. mult.)}\text{.} \end{align*}
