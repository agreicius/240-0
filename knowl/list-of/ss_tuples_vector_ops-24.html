Example 1.1.24. Linear combination: matrices.

Express \(\boldzero_{2\times 3}\) as a nontrivial linear combination of \(A=\begin{amatrix} 1\amp 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1\amp 1 \end{amatrix}\) and \(B=\begin{amatrix} 2\amp 2\amp 2\\ 2\amp 2\amp 2 \end{amatrix}\text{.}\)
🔗
Solution.
It is easy to see that \(B=2A\text{,}\) and hence
\begin{gather*} \boldzero_{2\times 3}=2A+\, -B=2A+(-1)B\text{.} \end{gather*}
More explicitly, we have
\begin{align*} \begin{amatrix} 0\amp 0\amp 0\\ 0\amp 0\amp 0 \end{amatrix} \amp =\begin{amatrix} 2\amp 2\amp 2\\ 2\amp 2\amp 2 \end{amatrix} +\begin{amatrix}[rrr] -2\amp -2\amp -2\\ -2\amp -2\amp -2 \end{amatrix}\\ \amp =2\begin{amatrix} 1\amp 1\amp 1\\ 1\amp 1\amp 1 \end{amatrix}+(-1)\begin{amatrix} 2\amp 2\amp 2\\ 2\amp 2\amp 2 \end{amatrix}\\ \amp =2A+(-1)B\text{.} \end{align*}
This is not the only nontrivial linear combination yielding \(\boldzero_{2\times 3}\text{.}\) In fact we have
\begin{equation*} \boldzero_{2\times 3}=(2r)A+(-r)B \end{equation*}
for any scalar \(r\in \R\) (including \(r=0\)).
