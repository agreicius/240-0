Example 1.1.12. Digital image as \(3\)-dimensional array.

Consider a digital image consisting of a \(1027\times 768\) array of pixels, each of which displays a color specified by a triple of real numbers \((r,g,b)\) that lists the intensities of the red, blue, and green color channels at that pixel. The image can be stored as a tuple \((a_{i,j,k})_{(i,j,k)\in I}\text{,}\) where
\begin{equation*} I=\{(i,j,k)\mid 1\leq i\leq 1027, 1\leq j\leq 768, 1\leq k\leq 3\} \end{equation*}
and \(a_{i,j,k}\) is the intensity of the \(k\)-th color channel (1=red, 2=green, 3=blue) at the pixel in the \((i,j)\)-th position. Our image is thus a \(3\)-dimensional array of size \(1027\times 768\times 3\text{.}\) See Sage Example 2 for a Sage demonstration of this idea.
🔗
Similarly to matrices, we can envision our \(3\)-dimensional array \((a_{i,j,k})_{(i,j,k)\in I}\) as a \(3\)-d box array of data consisting of three flat sheets stacked vertically: the first sheet consists of the entries \(a_{i,j,1}\) arranged as a grid, and similarly, the second and third sheets consist of the entries \(a_{i,j,2}\) and \(a_{i,j,3}\text{,}\) respectively. The mathematical usefulness of such a conception is up for debate. However, we can give some (somewhat dubious) physical intuition in this case as follows. Imagine the first sheet being a red-tinted translucent that colors the \((i,j)\)-th pixel red with intensity \(a_{i,j,1}\) for all all pairs \((i,j)\text{.}\) Similarly, imagine the second and third sheets as green- and blue-tinted translucents that color the \((i,j)\)-th pixel green (or blue) with intensity \(a_{i,j,2}\) (or \(a_{i,j,3}\)). Now take all three sheets, stack them on top of one another, shine a light through them, and voilà, our image appears!
